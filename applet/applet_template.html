Model Lab

A 90-minute, install-free tour of tree models, neural networks and GPTs. Every model trains in your browser tab on real liver-microsome clearance data from Biogen.

The question

Given a few physical properties of a molecule, how fast do human liver microsomes (HLM) clear it? The target is log10 HLM intrinsic clearance, so higher means the compound is metabolised faster.

Data: 3,087 compounds with HLM measurements from the public Biogen ADME set (Fang et al., J. Chem. Inf. Model. 2023; github.com/molecularinformatics/Computational-ADME). Test compounds are chosen by Murcko scaffold, so whole chemical series are held out, as in a real project.

Agenda

  1. 0–10 min
    Start. The question, the data, what a train/test split is and why the scaffold split is stricter.
  2. 10–35 min
    Trees. One decision tree, why it overfits, then random forests (averaging many trees) and gradient boosting (trees that fix each other's mistakes).
  3. 35–60 min
    Neural nets. A multi-layer perceptron on the same table. Watch train and validation loss diverge, then fight overfitting with dropout and early stopping.
  4. 60–80 min
    GPT. Train a tiny decoder-only transformer on SMILES text. Generate molecules and look at what attention attends to.
  5. 80–90 min
    Compare. Put every model on one table and discuss against the paper.

Controls

What each one is

Decision tree: a chain of yes/no questions on features ("is logP > 3.1?"). The prediction is the average of the training compounds that land in the same leaf.

Random forest: many deep trees, each trained on a random resample of compounds and a random subset of features at every split. Averaging cancels their individual errors.

Gradient boosting: shallow trees added one after another, each trained on the errors the current ensemble still makes.

Controls

What it is

Layers of output = max(0, W·input + b) stacked on each other. Gradient descent adjusts every weight to shrink the squared prediction error. The inputs are standardised (mean 0, spread 1) because networks, unlike trees, are sensitive to feature scale.

The network is trained on 85% of the training compounds. The remaining 15% (validation) tells you when to stop. The test scaffolds are never touched.

Train a tiny GPT

A GPT is a decoder-only transformer trained for one job: given the tokens so far, predict the next one. Here the text is SMILES strings of 1,500 training compounds. It never sees a clearance value.

Sample molecules

"Syntax ok" means brackets and ring numbers balance. It is a quick check, not full chemical validation. The notebook version uses RDKit for that.

Next-token loss

Loss starts near ln(vocab) because the model guesses uniformly, and falls as it learns SMILES grammar: rings open and close, brackets pair, atoms follow plausible neighbours.

Where does each token look?

Row = the token being processed. Column = an earlier token it attends to. Brighter = more weight. Cells above the diagonal are masked, since a GPT may not look at the future. Shown: last layer, averaged over heads.

Try: Generate at temperature 0.3, then 1.6. What changes about validity and variety?
Try: Train for 100 steps, then 1000. When do ring closures start appearing in pairs?
Discuss: ChatGPT is this recipe with a vastly larger network, vastly more text and extra tuning to follow instructions. What would you expect to change if this model saw 100 million molecules?

Discussion

  1. Which family won in your session? Would it win on a different split or random seed? What would you need to run to claim one family is better?
  2. Why do tree ensembles so often beat neural networks on tables of a few thousand rows?
  3. The GPT scores lowest at predicting clearance. Is it a bad model, or is regression the wrong job for it on 2,500 molecules?
  4. Fang et al. evaluate on compounds measured after the models were built. Which of today's splits comes closest, and what does it still miss?
  5. Assay replicates disagree by some amount. How does that cap the R² any model can reach?
